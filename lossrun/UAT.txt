import struct, datetime

def _dto(raw):
    # DATETIMEOFFSET: year, month, day, hour, minute, second, nanoseconds, offset hours, offset minutes
    y, mo, d, h, mi, s, ns, oh, om = struct.unpack("<6hI2h", raw)
    tz = datetime.timezone(datetime.timedelta(hours=oh, minutes=om))
    return datetime.datetime(y, mo, d, h, mi, s, ns // 1000, tzinfo=tz)

Banks such as Capital One don't show EBITDA (or EBIT) on purpose. It's a design choice, not missing data.

Why EBITDA doesn't fit banks

Interest is a bank's main cost, not a financing cost. A bank earns money on the spread between the interest it charges on loans and the interest it pays on deposits and borrowings. EBITDA adds interest expense back, which removes the bank's largest cost of doing business, so the result doesn't mean much.
Bank income statements have no "operating income" line. They go from net interest income, fee income, credit loss provisions and operating expenses straight to pre-tax income.
Interest coverage (EBITDA or EBIT / interest) doesn't measure a bank's ability to pay its debts. For banks that is judged by capital, liquidity and funding instead.
What we show for banks instead

Profitability: Net Income, ROA, ROE, Net Interest Margin, Fee Income and its growth.
Capital and liquidity: CET1 Ratio, Tier 1 Capital Ratio, Liquidity Coverage Ratio.
Credit quality: Nonperforming Loan Ratio, Allowance for Loan Losses / Loans, CRE Loans / Total Loans.
Funding: Total Deposits, Deposit Growth, Uninsured Deposits / Total Deposits, Unrealized Securities Losses / Equity.
If you'd like a bank equivalent of EBITDA, the standard measure is Pre-Provision Net Revenue (PPNR): net interest income plus fee income, minus operating expenses. It shows core earnings before credit losses and is the measure regulators use in bank stress tests. A related measure is the Efficiency Ratio (operating expenses / revenue), which shows cost control. We can add one or both for all banks if that would be useful. Just let us know.

Yes. Insider trading is tracked for every company from its SEC Form 4 filings, which officers and directors must file within two business days of a trade. Today we collect about 113,000 trades across 276 companies.

What you see in the app today

Insider cluster selling appears as a risk item under Governance & Leadership → Insider Trading. It is raised when 3 or more officers or directors sell on the open market within 30 days.
It counts open-market sales only. Pre-scheduled sales under a Rule 10b5-1 trading plan are left out (about two-thirds of all sales), and so are option exercises, tax withholding, awards and gifts. This keeps it to discretionary selling.
Around events: the item is raised to Warning when a sharp stock drop, or a drop after an earnings miss, follows within 90 days. In other words, insiders sold before bad news. So far there are about 775 such items across 153 companies, and 115 of them are Warnings.
These items also feed the AI-generated Key Insights and Strategic Risk sections.

Tighter or looser thresholds, for example 2 insiders instead of 3, or a different window, either for everyone or for a particular industry.
Sales ahead of specific events, for example insiders selling within N days before an earnings release, a restatement, a cybersecurity incident, an executive departure, or an M&A announcement. This would be a new type of alert.
Large single-insider sales, for example the CEO or CFO selling more than X% of their holdings in a quarter.
A trade-by-trade list (who, when, how many shares, price, plan or not) on the company page.
